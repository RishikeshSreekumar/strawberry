# Course: Mechanics II, Momentum, Rotation and Gravitation

**Slug:** `momentum-rotation-gravitation` (a physics course alongside `motion-forces-energy`; same block schema, same seed-script pattern as the maths courses. File/quiz prefix `mrg`, exports `mrgChapter{N}Lessons`, content files `src/modules/content/mrg-chapter-{0..5}-content.ts`.)

**Purpose:** Take a student who can already draw a free-body diagram and apply $\vec F = m\vec a$ to a single block (Mechanics I), and show them that the same three laws, applied to *systems* and to *extended bodies*, give three new conservation laws and the whole of rigid-body motion and gravitation. Every result is grown from Newton's laws plus one idea per chapter (a weighted average, action–reaction pairs, $\sum m r^2$, $\vec r\times\vec F$, $v = \omega R$, the inverse-square law), and every chapter pays off in JEE Main and JEE Advanced numericals at CBSE Class 11 depth.

**End state:** Given any mechanics problem involving several bodies, spinning bodies or planets, the student can pick the right tool and reconstruct it from:

```text
Newton's laws for a system  →  F_ext = M a_cm              (centre of mass)
                            →  F_ext = 0 ⇒ p conserved      (collisions, explosions, rockets)
apply to a spinning body    →  τ_ext = I α,  K = ½Iω²       (moment of inertia, torque)
                            →  τ_ext = 0 ⇒ L conserved      (skaters, rolling, impulses)
F = GMm/r²                  →  g(r), V(r), orbits, escape   (gravitation)
```

They should be able to say *why* the COM of an exploding shell keeps its parabola, *why* a solid sphere beats a ring down any incline whatever their masses, *why* angular momentum about the contact point is conserved when a skidding ball starts to roll, and *why* escape speed does not depend on the direction of launch.

**Three threads run through every chapter**
1. **Intuition + visualization:** every new idea appears first as something that moves (carts colliding with a centre-of-mass marker gliding past, arrows on a rolling wheel, bodies racing down a slope, a force-time pulse whose area fills in) before it appears as a formula.
2. **Derivation drill:** results are derived from a small core (Newton's three laws, the definition of COM, $K = \tfrac12\sum mv^2$, $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$, Newton's law of gravitation) and never stated cold. Each chapter's mastery lesson hides the formula sheet.
3. **JEE pay-off:** every lesson has at least one JEE-Main-style numerical with numbered steps and "*Why this step:*" notes; every mastery lesson includes JEE-Advanced-flavoured multi-step problems (collision followed by circular motion, cut body MI, rolling with slipping, orbit transfer).

**Conventions for content writers:** SI units; $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$ unless stated (say so in each lesson where $g$ appears); $G = 6.67\times10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$; Earth $R = 6400$ km, $M = 6\times10^{24}$ kg. Positive direction stated in every 1D problem. ω > 0 means clockwise in the rolling lab (the forward-rolling sense for motion to the right). Quiz ids `mrg{chapter}-{lesson}-q{n}` (e.g. `mrg1-5-q3`); the mastery lesson is lesson number `L` of its chapter and uses `mrg{C}-{L}-q{n}`.

## Chapter 0: Centre of Mass

**Chapter slug:** `centre-of-mass`

**Purpose:** introduce the centre of mass as a mass-weighted average position (linking straight back to the section formula of Vector Algebra), compute it for particle systems, continuous bodies and bodies with holes, and prove the key dynamical fact $\vec F_{\text{ext}} = M\vec a_{cm}$: internal forces never move the COM.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 0.1 | `why-a-centre-of-mass` | Why a Centre of Mass? | A spinning, tumbling body still has one point that follows the simple path | Hook: a spanner thrown across ice (or a diver) tumbles wildly, yet one point on it traces a clean straight line (parabola in air). Two-particle COM on a line derived as the weighted average $x_{cm} = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2}{m_1+m_2}$; it divides the join in the *inverse* ratio of the masses ($r_1 : r_2 = m_2 : m_1$). Interactive: `vec-canvas-2d` (mode `section`) with A and B as the two masses; the $m$, $n$ sliders are $m_B$, $m_A$ (the nearer point gets the bigger weight). Worked examples: Earth–Moon barycentre (inside Earth, about 4700 km from its centre with $M_E = 81M_m$, separation $3.84\times10^5$ km); 2 kg and 3 kg at the ends of a 1 m light rod. Misconception killed: "the COM is closer to the lighter mass" (it is closer to the heavier one) |
| 0.2 | `com-of-particle-systems` | Centre of Mass of a System of Particles | $\vec r_{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i\vec r_i}{\sum m_i}$, component by component | Vector form and the $x$, $y$, $z$ component forms. Choosing the origin at a heavy mass or on a symmetry line to kill terms. Worked examples: four masses at corners of a square (1, 2, 3, 4 kg), three equal masses at the vertices of a triangle (centroid), water molecule (O–H 0.96 Å, angle 104.5°, COM on the bisector). Grouping: replace a sub-system by its total mass at its own COM. Interactive: `vec-canvas-2d` (mode `triangle`) for three equal masses. Misconception killed: "the COM must lie inside the material" (a ring, an L-shape, a boomerang) |
| 0.3 | `com-of-continuous-bodies` | Continuous Bodies: Integration | Replace $\sum m_i x_i$ by $\int x\,dm$ with $dm = \lambda\,dx$, $\sigma\,dA$ or $\rho\,dV$ | Derivations: uniform rod ($L/2$), non-uniform rod $\lambda = kx$ ($2L/3$), semicircular ring ($2R/\pi$), semicircular disc ($4R/3\pi$), right circular cone ($h/4$ from base), solid hemisphere ($3R/8$), hollow hemisphere ($R/2$), triangular plate (centroid). Strategy callout: pick the element that makes every point of it equally far along the axis (strips, rings, discs). Interactive: `transform-playground` showing density $\lambda \propto x^n$ with slider $n$ (the COM formula $\frac{n+1}{n+2}L$ as a quiz follow-up). Results table for reference in later lessons. Misconception killed: "the COM of a semicircular ring and a semicircular disc are the same point" |
| 0.4 | `composite-bodies-and-cavities` | Composite Bodies and Cavities | A hole is a negative mass at the hole's centre | Combining pieces (L-shaped plate, T-shaped lamina, rod + sphere) by grouping. Removing mass: disc of radius $R$ with a hole of radius $R/2$ touching the rim (COM shifts $R/6$ away from the hole). Square plate with a quarter removed. Interactive: `vec-canvas-2d` (mode `section`, `allowExternal: true`): the remaining body's COM lies on the far side, an external division. Misconception killed: "removing a piece moves the COM towards the hole" |
| 0.5 | `motion-of-the-com` | Motion of the Centre of Mass | $M\vec a_{cm} = \vec F_{\text{ext}}$: internal forces cancel in pairs | Derivation: differentiate $M\vec r_{cm}$ twice, sum Newton's second law over particles, Newton's third law kills internal forces. $\vec v_{cm} = \vec P/M$. Exploding projectile: fragments scatter but the COM completes the original parabola (JEE classic: shell explodes at the top into two equal parts, one falls vertically; where does the other land? at $1.5\times$ the range). Interactive: `mrg-collision-lab` (mode `explosion`, `showCom: true`, `graph: "momentum"`): spring pushes the carts apart; COM marker keeps gliding. Misconception killed: "an explosion changes the path of the COM" |
| 0.6 | `when-the-com-stays-put` | When the Centre of Mass Stays Put | No external force along $x$ and initially at rest ⇒ $\Delta x_{cm} = 0$, so $\sum m_i\Delta x_i = 0$ | Man walking on a boat (boat moves back by $\frac{m}{m+M}L$), frog jumping off a plank, block sliding down a smooth wedge on a smooth floor (wedge displacement), two skaters pulling a rope (meet at the COM), balloon with a climbing man. Worked examples with numbered steps; "choose ground displacements, not relative ones" callout. Interactive: `mrg-collision-lab` (mode `explosion`, `controls: ["m1", "m2", "energy"]`, `frame: "ground"`). Misconception killed: "the man walks $L$ relative to the ground" (he walks $L$ relative to the boat) |
| 0.7 | `chapter-0-mastery` | Chapter 0 Mastery | Locate the COM and use its motion, with no formula sheet | Mastery (8–12 quizzes): COM of particle systems, rod with $\lambda = a + bx$, semicircular disc and ring, disc with a hole, composite lamina, exploding projectile landing point, boat–man displacement, wedge–block displacement, velocity of COM from momenta, two particles released from rest under mutual attraction meet at the COM |

## Chapter 1: Momentum, Impulse and Collisions

**Chapter slug:** `momentum-impulse-collisions`

**Purpose:** rewrite Newton's second law as $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$, get conservation of momentum from the third law, handle impulsive forces and variable mass, and then classify and solve every JEE collision (1D elastic and inelastic, coefficient of restitution, oblique collisions) with the centre-of-mass frame as the unifying picture.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 1.1 | `momentum-and-its-conservation` | Momentum and Its Conservation | $\vec F_{\text{ext}} = d\vec P/dt$, so $\vec F_{\text{ext}} = 0 \Rightarrow \vec P$ constant | Hook: a skater throws a heavy ball and slides backwards. $\vec p = m\vec v$; Newton's second law in momentum form. Derivation of conservation from the third law for two bodies, then for a system. Component-wise conservation (only along directions with no external force). Recoil of a gun (5 kg gun, 20 g bullet at 500 m/s ⇒ 2 m/s), man jumping off a cart, bullet fired from a block on a smooth surface. Interactive: `mrg-collision-lab` (mode `explosion`, `graph: "momentum"`): $p_1$ and $p_2$ change, $p_1 + p_2$ is a flat line. Misconception killed: "if momentum is conserved, kinetic energy must be too" |
| 1.2 | `impulse` | Impulse and Impulsive Forces | $\vec J = \int\vec F\,dt = \Delta\vec p$: the area under $F$–$t$ | Derivation from $F = dp/dt$. Average force $\bar F = J/\Delta t$. Catching a cricket ball (why hands move back), airbags, a ball hitting a wall at an angle (only the normal component reverses). Impulsive forces dwarf gravity and friction during the contact ($\bar F$ vs $mg$ readout), impulsive tension when a string jerks taut. Interactive: `mrg-collision-lab` (mode `wall`, pulses `triangle`, then `rectangle`/`half-sine`): same area, different peak; contact-time slider. Worked example: 0.15 kg ball at 20 m/s rebounds at 15 m/s, contact 5 ms ⇒ $J = 5.25$ N s, $\bar F = 1050$ N. Misconception killed: "a softer landing means a smaller impulse" (same impulse, spread over more time, so a smaller force) |
| 1.3 | `variable-mass-and-rockets` | Variable Mass: Conveyor Belts and Rockets | When mass flows in or out, $\vec F_{\text{ext}} = m\frac{d\vec v}{dt} - \vec u_{\text{rel}}\frac{dm}{dt}$ | Sand falling on a moving conveyor belt ($F = v\,dm/dt$ to keep it moving, power $v^2\,dm/dt$, half of which becomes heat), chain falling onto a table (force $3\times$ weight of fallen part), water jet on a wall ($F = \rho A v^2$). Rocket: thrust $u\,|dm/dt|$, derivation of $v = u\ln\frac{m_0}{m} - gt$. Interactive: `transform-playground` with $v/u = \ln\frac{1}{1-f}$ against burnt fraction $f$ and slider $u$. Worked JEE example: exhaust speed 2 km/s, burn rate needed to lift a 5000 kg rocket with $a = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$ (50 kg/s). Misconception killed: "a rocket pushes against the air" |
| 1.4 | `elastic-and-inelastic-collisions` | Elastic and Inelastic Collisions in One Dimension | Momentum always; kinetic energy only if elastic | Classification by KE: elastic, inelastic, perfectly inelastic. 1D elastic derivation from momentum + KE, factorising to "relative velocity reverses", then $v_1' = \frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}u_1 + \frac{2m_2}{m_1+m_2}u_2$. Special cases: equal masses swap velocities (Newton's cradle), heavy on light (light one flies off at $2u$), light on heavy (bounces back). Perfectly inelastic: common velocity and KE loss $\tfrac12\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}(u_1-u_2)^2$. Interactive: `mrg-collision-lab` (mode `collision`, `e` initial 1 then 0, `graph: "energy"`): the KE dip at maximum compression. Misconception killed: "in a perfectly inelastic collision all the kinetic energy is lost" (only the part relative to the COM) |
| 1.5 | `coefficient-of-restitution` | The Coefficient of Restitution | $e = \frac{\text{speed of separation}}{\text{speed of approach}}$ along the line of impact | Compression and restitution phases; $e = J_{\text{restitution}}/J_{\text{compression}}$; common velocity at maximum compression. General 1D results with $e$ and KE loss $\tfrac12\mu(1-e^2)u_{\text{rel}}^2$. Ball dropped from $h$: rebound heights $e^{2n}h$, total distance $h\frac{1+e^2}{1-e^2}$, total time $\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}\frac{1+e}{1-e}$. Interactive: `mrg-collision-lab` (mode `collision`, `graph: "velocity"`, e slider), then the COM-frame toggle: each velocity just reverses and shrinks by $e$. Second interactive: `transform-playground` with bounce heights $h\,r^{2n}$ (parameter named `r`, not `e`, since `e` is a reserved constant in the expression evaluator). Misconceptions killed: "$e$ is a property of one ball" (it belongs to the pair) and "$e = v/u$ for each ball separately" |
| 1.6 | `collisions-in-two-dimensions` | Oblique Collisions | Conserve momentum in both components; $e$ acts only along the line of centres | Glancing collision of equal masses, one at rest, elastic ⇒ they separate at 90° (proof from $\vec u = \vec v_1 + \vec v_2$ and $u^2 = v_1^2 + v_2^2$). Line of impact: resolve along and perpendicular to it, tangential components unchanged (smooth spheres), apply 1D results along the normal. Ball hitting a smooth floor at angle $\theta$ with restitution $e$ (rebound angle $\tan\theta' = \tan\theta/e$). Interactives: `vec-canvas-2d` (mode `add`, parallelogram on) for $\vec p_1' + \vec p_2' = \vec p_1$; `vec-canvas-2d` (mode `dot`, `showPerpendicular: true`) resolving velocity along the line of centres. Misconception killed: "$e$ applies to the whole velocity" |
| 1.7 | `collision-problem-toolkit` | Collision Problems: The JEE Toolkit | Split the event into stages: impulsive stage (momentum) then non-impulsive stage (energy) | Ballistic pendulum ($v = \frac{m+M}{m}\sqrt{2gh}$), bullet passing through a block, block–spring–block (maximum compression when velocities are equal: $\tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12\mu u^2$), collision on a smooth wedge (only horizontal momentum), particle hitting a hanging bob then completing a vertical circle. Strategy callout: "momentum across the collision, energy before and after it, never energy across it". Interactives: `function-machine` for the ballistic pendulum ($h$ in, bullet speed out), `mrg-collision-lab` (mode `collision`, `e` 0, `graph: "energy"`, `frame: "com"`) as the spring-block analogue. Misconception killed: "use energy conservation through the whole ballistic pendulum" |
| 1.8 | `chapter-1-mastery` | Chapter 1 Mastery | Momentum and impulse, from scratch | Mastery (10–12 quizzes): recoil, impulse from a force-time graph, average force from rebound, conveyor belt power, rocket thrust, elastic 1D special cases, $e$ from before/after velocities, KE loss, bounce series, oblique elastic equal masses (90°), floor rebound angle, ballistic pendulum and block–spring–block (Advanced-style two-stage problems) |

## Chapter 2: Rotational Kinematics and Moment of Inertia

**Chapter slug:** `rotational-kinematics-moment-of-inertia`

**Purpose:** describe rigid-body rotation about a fixed axis with angular variables and their vector nature, relate them to linear velocity and acceleration of each point, and build moment of inertia from $\sum m r^2$: for discrete systems, standard continuous bodies, and via the parallel and perpendicular axis theorems.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 2.1 | `rigid-bodies-and-rotation` | Rigid Bodies and Rotation | Every point of a rigid body turns through the same angle; speed grows with distance from the axis | Rigid body, pure translation vs pure rotation vs both. $\theta$, $\omega$, $\alpha$; the right-hand rule makes $\vec\omega$ a vector along the axis. $\vec v = \vec\omega\times\vec r$, $a_t = \alpha r$, $a_c = \omega^2 r$, total $a = r\sqrt{\alpha^2 + \omega^4}$. Interactive: `mrg-rolling-lab` (mode `velocities`, `v` initial 0, `showParts: false`): pure rotation, velocity arrows proportional to radius. Worked example: fan blade tip at 1200 rpm, 0.5 m (62.8 m/s). Misconception killed: "every point of a rotating body has the same speed" |
| 2.2 | `rotational-kinematics` | Equations of Rotational Kinematics | Constant $\alpha$: $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$, $\theta = \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2$, $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta$ | Derivation by integration, analogy table with linear kinematics. Revolutions vs radians. Variable $\alpha$ with calculus ($\alpha = \omega\,d\omega/d\theta$). Number of turns before stopping; revolutions in the $n$th second. Interactive: `transform-playground` with $\theta(t) = \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2$, sliders `w` and `a`. Worked example: wheel from 600 rpm to rest in 10 s ($\alpha = -2\pi$ rad/s², 50 revolutions). Misconception killed: "$\theta$ from the equations is in revolutions" (it is radians; divide by $2\pi$) |
| 2.3 | `moment-of-inertia` | Moment of Inertia | $K = \tfrac12\left(\sum m_i r_i^2\right)\omega^2$ defines $I = \sum m_i r_i^2$ | Derivation from the KE of rotating particles. Rotational inertia depends on mass *and* its distribution *and* the axis. Radius of gyration $k$. Discrete systems: masses on a light rod about different axes; square of four masses about a side, a diagonal, the centre. Interactive: `transform-playground` with $I(x) = m_1x^2 + m_2(L-x)^2$ against axis position $x$, sliders `m1`, `m2`: the minimum sits at the COM (setting up 2.5). Misconception killed: "moment of inertia is a fixed property of a body, like mass" |
| 2.4 | `moment-of-inertia-of-standard-bodies` | Moments of Inertia of Standard Bodies | Slice the body into elements whose distance from the axis is known and integrate | Derivations: rod about centre ($ML^2/12$) and end ($ML^2/3$), ring ($MR^2$), disc ($MR^2/2$) via rings, hollow and solid cylinder, rectangular plate, solid sphere ($\tfrac25MR^2$) via discs, hollow sphere ($\tfrac23MR^2$) via rings. Summary table with $k^2/R^2$ values (1, ½, 2/5, 2/3) that return in Chapter 4. Misconception killed: "a long cylinder has a bigger $I$ about its axis than a flat disc of the same mass and radius" (length along the axis does not matter) |
| 2.5 | `parallel-and-perpendicular-axis-theorems` | Parallel and Perpendicular Axis Theorems | $I = I_{cm} + Md^2$; for a lamina $I_z = I_x + I_y$ | Derivations: parallel axis by expanding $\sum m(\vec r + \vec d)^2$ (the cross term vanishes because $\sum m\vec r = 0$ about the COM); perpendicular axis from $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$ for a flat body. Uses: rod about end, disc about a tangent in its plane ($\tfrac54MR^2$) and perpendicular to it ($\tfrac32MR^2$), ring about diameter ($\tfrac12MR^2$), square plate about a diagonal. Interactive: `transform-playground` with $I/M = k_{cm}^2 + d^2$ against $d$. | 2.6 | `composite-and-cut-bodies` | Composite, Cut and Hollowed Bodies | $I$ adds for pieces about the same axis; removed parts subtract | Composite: rod + disc pendulum, T-shape, dumbbell. Removal: disc with a concentric hole, disc with an off-centre hole (parallel axis on the removed disc). Cut sectors: a quarter disc of mass $M$ still has $MR^2/2$ about the original axis. | 2.7 | `chapter-2-mastery` | Chapter 2 Mastery | Angular kinematics and $I$ from scratch | Mastery (8–12 quizzes): $\omega$, $\alpha$, turns before stopping, total acceleration of a rim point, discrete $I$ about various axes, standard bodies, radius of gyration, parallel/perpendicular axis combos (disc about tangent, ring about tangent), cut and hollowed bodies, the axis that minimises $I$ |

## Chapter 4: Angular Momentum and Rolling

**Chapter slug:** `angular-momentum-rolling`

**Purpose:** define angular momentum for particles and rigid bodies, derive $\vec\tau = d\vec L/dt$ and its conservation, then treat rolling as translation plus rotation: the rolling constraint, rolling down inclines, the slip-to-roll transition, and impulses on rigid bodies.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 4.1 | `angular-momentum` | Angular Momentum | $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$; for rotation about a fixed axis $L = I\omega$; $\vec\tau = d\vec L/dt$ | Particle: $L = mvr_\perp$, constant for a free particle about any point (lever arm fixed). **Purpose:** state Newton's law of gravitation and the shell theorem, use them to find how $g$ varies with height, depth and latitude, define gravitational field and potential for standard mass distributions, and apply energy and angular momentum conservation to escape speed, satellite orbits and Kepler's laws.
