# Course: Mechanics I, Motion, Forces and Energy

**Slug:** `motion-forces-energy` (the first of five JEE physics courses, alongside `momentum-rotation-gravitation`, `oscillations-waves-thermal`, `electricity-and-magnetism` and `optics-and-modern-physics`; same block schema, same seed-script pattern as the maths courses.)

**Purpose:** Build the habit that every mechanics problem is solved from a picture first (a track, a trajectory, a free-body diagram, an energy bar chart) and from a tiny set of laws second: the definitions of velocity and acceleration, Newton's three laws, and the work-energy theorem. Then build the fluency CBSE Class 11 and JEE Main/Advanced expect: units and dimensions, error analysis, kinematic graphs, projectiles, relative motion, connected bodies, friction, circular dynamics, and work, energy and power.

**End state:** Given any Mechanics I question, the student can reconstruct the answer from:

```text
units + dimensions   → check every answer before trusting it
x(t) → v = dx/dt → a = dv/dt          (and back again by areas)
vectors: resolve along the right axes, motion in x and y is independent
free-body diagram → ΣF = ma along each axis (+ pseudo force in an accelerating frame)
constraints (strings, contact, friction ≤ μN, circular: net inward force = mv²/r)
W_all forces = ΔK   →   conservative forces: W = −ΔU, so K + U is constant
```

They should never need a memorised list of "results": they should be able to say *why* the range is largest at 45°, *why* static friction is not always $\mu_s N$, *why* a bob needs $\sqrt{5gR}$ at the bottom to loop the loop, and *why* the area under $F$-$x$ is work.

**Three threads run through every chapter**
1. **Intuition + visualization:** every new idea appears first as something that moves (a particle on a track with live graphs, a trajectory with its velocity arrows, a block whose friction arrow grows and then snaps, a bob whose string goes slack) before it appears as a formula.
2. **Derivation drill:** results are derived from a small core (definitions of $v$ and $a$, Newton's laws, $W = \int \vec F\cdot d\vec s$), never stated cold. Each chapter's mastery lesson hides the formula sheet.
3. **JEE pay-off:** every lesson carries at least one JEE-Main-style numerical with numbered steps and a "*Why this step:*" note; every mastery lesson finishes with JEE-Advanced-flavoured multi-step problems. $g = 10$ m/s² throughout unless a question says otherwise (say so in each chapter's first lesson).

## Chapter 0: Units, Dimensions and Measurement

**Purpose:** set up the language of physics: SI units and conversions, dimensional formulae and what dimensional analysis can (and cannot) do, significant figures, errors and their propagation, and a fast vectors-for-physics recap (resolution, resultant, relative vectors) that Chapters 1–5 lean on.

## Chapter 1: Motion in a Straight Line

**Purpose:** kinematics in one dimension as three linked graphs: position, velocity, acceleration. Slopes go down the chain, areas come back up. Derive the three equations of uniformly accelerated motion from the $v$-$t$ graph, handle free fall, and finish with the calculus of non-uniform acceleration.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 1.1 | `position-displacement-and-distance` | Position, Displacement and Distance | A frame and an origin turn motion into a function $x(t)$; displacement is change in position, distance is path length | Hook: a car goes 5 km east then 3 km west: odometer says 8 km, displacement is 2 km east. Frame of reference, point-object idealisation, sign convention on a line. Average speed vs average velocity. Interactive: `mfe-motion-lab` (mode `line`, $u = 10$, $a = -2$, duration 10) showing the turnaround at $t = 5$ s and distance racing ahead of displacement. Worked examples: average speed for equal distances at $v_1, v_2$ (harmonic mean $\frac{2v_1v_2}{v_1+v_2}$) vs equal times (arithmetic mean). Misconceptions killed: "average speed is the magnitude of average velocity" and "average speed is the average of the speeds" |
| 1.2 | `velocity-and-position-time-graphs` | Velocity and Position–Time Graphs | Instantaneous velocity is the slope of the tangent to $x$-$t$ | From average velocity (secant slope) to instantaneous velocity (tangent slope) as $\Delta t \to 0$. Interactive: `secant-explorer` with $x = 5t^2$ (expr `5*x^2`, $x_1 = 1$), then `mfe-motion-lab` (mode `line`, graphs `["x"]`, showTangent). Reading $x$-$t$ graphs: straight line = uniform velocity, curving up/down, steeper = faster, a horizontal tangent = momentarily at rest, two graphs crossing = the bodies meet. Worked examples: velocity from a sketch; which of four $x$-$t$ graphs is impossible (vertical segment: infinite speed; two $x$ at one $t$). Misconception killed: "the $x$-$t$ graph is the path of the particle" |
| 1.3 | `acceleration-and-velocity-time-graphs` | Acceleration and Velocity–Time Graphs | $a$ is the slope of $v$-$t$; the area under $v$-$t$ is the displacement | Interactive: `mfe-motion-lab` (mode `line`, graphs `["v","a"]`, showArea) with $u = -6$, $a = 2$: the shaded area is negative then positive; displacement vs distance from the areas. Sign of $a$ vs speeding up/slowing down: speeding up iff $v$ and $a$ have the same sign. Area under $a$-$t$ = change in velocity. Worked examples: displacement and distance from a trapezium-shaped $v$-$t$ graph (car accelerating, cruising, braking); acceleration from a piecewise $v$-$t$ graph. Misconceptions killed: "negative acceleration means slowing down" and "$v = 0$ means $a = 0$" |
| 1.4 | `equations-of-uniformly-accelerated-motion` | Equations of Uniformly Accelerated Motion | Constant $a$ → straight $v$-$t$ line → three equations from one trapezium | Derive $v = u + at$ (slope), $s = ut + \tfrac12at^2$ (trapezium area), $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ (eliminate $t$), and $s = \tfrac{u+v}{2}t$. Displacement in the $n$th second $s_n = u + \tfrac{a}{2}(2n - 1)$ derived as $s(n) - s(n-1)$. Interactive: `mfe-motion-lab` (mode `line`) with readout $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$ checked live. Worked JEE examples: braking distance ∝ $u^2$ (doubling speed quadruples stopping distance); two cars and a meeting point; stopping distance with reaction time; a bullet losing half its speed in 3 cm penetrates how much further (1 cm). Misconception killed: "these equations work for any motion" (only constant $a$) |
| 1.5 | `free-fall-and-vertical-motion` | Free Fall and Vertical Motion | Near the Earth every body accelerates at $g$ downward, whether rising or falling | Galileo's odd-number rule: distances in successive seconds 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 (derived from $s_n$). Throw up with speed $u$: time of ascent $u/g$, max height $u^2/2g$, symmetry of speeds at the same height, total time $2u/g$. Sign convention callout (choose up positive and stick to it). Interactive: `mfe-motion-lab` (mode `line`, $x_0 = 0$, $u = 20$, $a$ pinned at $-10$, duration 4, label caption about "x is height"). Worked examples: ball thrown up from a 25 m tower at 20 m/s (time to hit ground from $-25 = 20t - 5t^2$, rejecting the negative root with reason); stone dropped from a rising balloon (it starts with the balloon's velocity); two balls thrown at an interval. Misconceptions killed: "at the highest point the acceleration is zero" and "a heavier body falls faster" |
| 1.6 | `calculus-of-kinematics` | Calculus of Kinematics | $v = \frac{dx}{dt}$, $a = \frac{dv}{dt} = v\frac{dv}{dx}$; integrate to go back | When $a$ is not constant. Given $x(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t$: $v$, $a$, turning points, distance in 0–4 s (split at $v = 0$). Interactive: `graph-explorer` on $x(t)$ (expr `x^3 - 6*x^2 + 9*x`, the slider variable is $t$) and `transform-playground` for $v(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 9$. Graph conversion drill: sketch $v$-$t$ and $a$-$t$ from an $x$-$t$ graph. Misconception killed: "$v\frac{dv}{dx}$ is a different acceleration" (chain rule on the same $a$) |
| 1.7 | `chapter-1-mastery` | Chapter 1 Mastery | Graphs, equations and calculus of 1D motion | Info callout "Chapter 1 in N lines". Mixed mastery (8–12): average speed traps, reading slopes and areas, $n$th-second displacement, stopping distances, vertical throw from a height, two-body meeting problems, $a(v)$ and $a(x)$ integration; JEE-Advanced flavour: a particle with $a = -kv^2$, or a lift-and-ball problem mixing relative motion with free fall |

| 2.4 | `relative-velocity` | Relative Velocity | $\vec v_{AB} = \vec v_A - \vec v_B$: how B sees A | 1D: two trains passing (time to cross = total length / relative speed), overtaking; a man running on a moving walkway. 2D: $\vec v_{AB}$ by vector subtraction, `vec-canvas-2d` (mode `subtract`, labels $\vec v_A$, $\vec v_B$). ## Chapter 3: Newton's Laws of Motion

**Purpose:** forces as interactions, the three laws, and the one method that solves every problem: isolate the body, draw its free-body diagram, choose axes, write $\sum F = ma$ per axis, add constraint equations. Then connected bodies, pulleys and non-inertial frames.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 3.1 | `force-inertia-and-the-first-law` | Force, Inertia and the First Law | Without a net force, velocity does not change | Hook: Aristotle's "moving things stop" vs Galileo's double inclined plane (friction is the hidden force). Fundamental forces vs contact forces (normal, tension, friction, spring) as their macroscopic faces. Inertia and mass; first law as the definition of an inertial frame. Examples: passengers jerked back when a bus starts; dusting a carpet; a coin on a card flicked away. Net force zero ⇒ constant velocity (not necessarily rest), with a worked example of a raindrop at terminal velocity. Misconception killed: "a moving body needs a force to keep it moving" |
| 3.2 | `second-law-momentum-and-impulse` | Second Law, Momentum and Impulse | $\vec F = \frac{d\vec p}{dt}$, which is $m\vec a$ for constant mass | Momentum, second law in momentum form, unit of force (newton) from $F = ma$. Impulse $\vec J = \int\vec F\,dt = \Delta\vec p$ = area under $F$-$t$: why a cricketer draws the hands back, why airbags work. Interactive: `graph-explorer` of a triangular force pulse (e.g. $F = 400 - 4000\lvert t - 0.1\rvert$ over $0 \le t \le 0.2$) to read the area. Worked JEE examples: ball of 0.15 kg hitting a wall at 20 m/s and rebounding at 15 m/s (impulse 5.25 N s, average force over 0.01 s); machine gun firing bullets (force = $n\,m\,v$ per second); force from a changing momentum $\vec p(t)$. | 3.3 | `third-law-and-free-body-diagrams` | Third Law and Free-Body Diagrams | Forces come in pairs acting on *different* bodies; an FBD shows only forces *on* one body | Third law pairs: book on a table (weight's pair is on the Earth, not the normal force). Normal force, tension (ideal massless string: same tension throughout), weight. FBD method callout in 5 steps. Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `incline`, muS and muK pinned at 0, force 0, showComponents): normal force is $mg\cos\theta$, not $mg$; the block accelerates at $g\sin\theta$. Worked examples: block on a smooth incline; person pushing a box: which forces go on which FBD; two blocks in contact pushed by $F$ (contact force $= \frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}F$). Misconceptions killed: "action and reaction cancel" and "the normal force always equals $mg$" |
| 3.4 | `equilibrium-of-concurrent-forces` | Equilibrium of Concurrent Forces | $\sum\vec F = 0$: the force polygon closes | Resolve along two perpendicular axes and set each sum to zero. Interactive: `vec-canvas-2d` (mode `add`) showing three forces tip-to-tail closing into a triangle. Lami's theorem $\frac{F_1}{\sin\alpha} = \frac{F_2}{\sin\beta} = \frac{F_3}{\sin\gamma}$ derived from the sine rule on the closed triangle. Worked examples: a 10 kg lamp hung by two strings at 30° and 60° (tensions 50 N and $50\sqrt3$ N); a block held on a smooth incline by a horizontal force ($F = mg\tan\theta$); a rope with a weight at its middle can never be perfectly horizontal. Misconception killed: "a string at a shallower angle carries less tension" (it carries more) |
| 3.5 | `connected-bodies-and-pulleys` | Connected Bodies and Pulleys | Same string ⇒ same tension and same magnitude of acceleration | Atwood machine: two FBDs, add the equations to eliminate $T$: $a = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)g}{m_1 + m_2}$, $T = \frac{2m_1m_2g}{m_1+m_2}$. Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `atwood`), then mode `table-pulley` with `mu` pinned at 0 (smooth table): $a = \frac{m_2g}{m_1+m_2}$. Worked JEE examples: three blocks pulled on a smooth floor (tensions in each string); block on an incline connected over a pulley to a hanging block (which way it moves). Misconception killed: "tension equals the hanging weight" (only in equilibrium) |
| 3.6 | `constraint-relations` | Constraint Relations | Inextensible strings and rigid contacts tie accelerations together | The string-length method: write the total length in terms of positions, differentiate twice. Movable pulley: $a_{\text{block}} = 2a_{\text{pulley}}$ derived, with the tension halving. Wedge constraint: a block on a free wedge keeps contact, so relative normal accelerations match. Worked JEE-Advanced examples: pulley systems with one movable pulley (masses 1 kg and 2 kg, find both accelerations and tension), a block on a wedge pushed by a horizontal force, the "virtual work" check $\sum T\cdot a = 0$ for ideal strings. Concept quizzes: which constraint equation is correct for a given diagram. Misconception killed: "all bodies on one string have the same acceleration" (not when pulleys move) |
| 3.7 | `pseudo-forces-and-non-inertial-frames` | Pseudo Forces and Non-Inertial Frames | In a frame accelerating at $\vec a_0$, add $-m\vec a_0$ to every body and use $\sum F = ma$ as usual | Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `lift`, toggle `frame`): from the ground $N - mg = ma$; from inside the lift add the pseudo force and $N = m(g + a)$ balances; free fall at $a = -g$ gives $N = 0$ (weightlessness). Pendulum in an accelerating car: $\tan\theta = a/g$, effective gravity $\sqrt{g^2 + a^2}$. Block on a smooth wedge accelerated so the block stays at rest ($a = g\tan\theta$). Worked JEE examples: apparent weight in a lift in each of four motion stages; a ball dropped inside an accelerating lift (relative acceleration $g + a$); water surface in an accelerating tanker tilts at $\tan^{-1}(a/g)$. ## Chapter 4: Friction and Circular Dynamics

**Purpose:** friction as a self-adjusting force with a ceiling ($f_s \le \mu_s N$) and a fixed kinetic value ($f_k = \mu_k N$), applied to inclines and stacked blocks; then circular motion dynamics: the net inward force must be $mv^2/r$, whoever supplies it (friction, normal force, tension, gravity).

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 4.1 | `static-and-kinetic-friction` | Static and Kinetic Friction | Static friction matches the push up to $\mu_s N$; after slipping, $f_k = \mu_k N$ | Hook: why it is harder to start pushing a heavy box than to keep it moving. Microscopic origin (welds at contact points), laws of friction, independence of contact area. Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `friction`, showGraph): drag $F$ and watch $f$ follow the 45° line, peak at $\mu_s mg$, drop to $\mu_k mg$. Pulling at an angle: $N = mg - F\sin\theta$, least force at $\tan\theta = \mu$ (derived by minimising). Rolling friction mentioned. Worked JEE examples: 5 kg block, $\mu_s = 0.5$, $\mu_k = 0.4$ pushed with 20 N (friction 20 N, not 25 N) then 30 N ($a = 2$ m/s²); least force to pull a block at an angle. Misconceptions killed: "friction is always $\mu N$" and "friction always opposes motion" (it opposes relative sliding, and can drive motion: walking, a box on an accelerating truck) |
| 4.2 | `friction-on-an-incline` | Friction on an Incline | Compare $mg\sin\theta$ with $\mu_s mg\cos\theta$ before assuming motion | Angle of repose $\tan\theta_r = \mu_s$ derived. Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `incline`): raise $\theta$ past $\theta_r$ and watch the block start sliding; add an applied force up or down the slope and watch friction flip direction. Force range to keep a block at rest: $mg(\sin\theta - \mu\cos\theta) \le F \le mg(\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta)$. Acceleration sliding down $g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$, up-then-down motion (different accelerations for the two legs, so different times). Worked JEE examples: block projected up a rough incline (time up vs time down); time to slide down a rough 45° incline is $n$ times that on a smooth one ($\mu = 1 - 1/n^2$ derived). Misconception killed: "friction on an incline always acts up the slope" |
| 4.3 | `block-on-block-problems` | Block-on-Block Problems | Assume they move together, check the friction needed against $\mu N$ | Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `block-on-block`, pushOn `bottom`, then `top`): find $F_{\text{crit}}$ where they begin to slip; the direction of friction on each block (third-law pair). Method callout: (1) assume common $a$, (2) find the friction the top block needs, (3) compare with $\mu_s N$, (4) if exceeded, redo with kinetic friction and separate accelerations. Worked JEE examples: 2 kg on 4 kg, $\mu = 0.3$, smooth floor, $F$ on the bottom = 10 N (together, $a = 5/3$ m/s²) and 30 N (slip: 3 m/s² and 6 m/s²); force on the top block; time for the top block to fall off a plank of length $L$; three-block stacks. Misconception killed: "friction on the top block opposes its motion" (it drives it when the bottom is pulled) |
| 4.4 | `dynamics-of-circular-motion` | Dynamics of Circular Motion | Centripetal force is not a new force; it is the net inward force | FBD for circular motion: sum the radial components and set equal to $mv^2/r$. Car on a flat curve: friction supplies $mv^2/r$, $v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu rg}$; interactive `mfe-force-lab` (mode `banking`, angle pinned at 0). Conical pendulum ($\tan\theta = v^2/rg$, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l\cos\theta/g}$); coin on a rotating turntable (slips first at the rim, $\omega_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu g/r}$); rotor ride (friction holds you up, $\mu \ge g/\omega^2r$); a stone whirled on a string. Centrifugal force as the pseudo force in the rotating frame, linked back to 3.7. Misconception killed: "a body in circular motion is pushed outward by a centrifugal force" (in the ground frame nothing pushes it outward; it tends to go straight) |
| 4.5 | `banking-of-roads` | Banking of Roads | Tilt the road so the normal force leans toward the centre | Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `banking`, showComponents): at $v_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta}$ friction is zero; faster needs friction down the slope, slower needs it up. Derive $v_{\max} = \sqrt{rg\frac{\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta}{\cos\theta - \mu\sin\theta}}$ and $v_{\min}$ from the two FBDs. Railway superelevation $h \approx \frac{v^2 b}{rg}$ for small angles; cyclist leaning ($\tan\theta = v^2/rg$); aircraft banking to turn. Worked JEE examples: design speed of a 30 m curve banked at 15°; max safe speed with $\mu = 0.2$; which way friction acts at a given speed. Misconception killed: "on a banked road $N = mg\cos\theta$" (it is larger: $N = m(g\cos\theta + \frac{v^2}{r}\sin\theta)$) |
| 4.6 | `motion-in-a-vertical-circle` | Motion in a Vertical Circle | Speed changes around the loop; the string must stay taut ($T \ge 0$) | Derive $v^2 = u^2 - 2gR(1 - \cos\theta)$ from the tangential equation $v\frac{dv}{ds} = -g\sin\theta$ (calculus of kinematics from 1.6; the energy view comes in Chapter 5). Radial equation $T = \frac{mv^2}{R} + mg\cos\theta$. Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `vertical-circle`): slide $\theta$ round the loop at $u = 8$, $R = 1$; drop $u$ below $\sqrt{5gR}$ and watch the string go slack at the predicted angle and the bob follow a parabola; below $\sqrt{2gR}$ it oscillates. Results: $u \ge \sqrt{5gR}$ to complete the loop with a string, $T_{\text{bottom}} - T_{\text{top}} = 6mg$; with a rigid rod or inside a tube, $u \ge \sqrt{4gR}$; car over a hump $N = mg - mv^2/r$ (loses contact at $v = \sqrt{gr}$); bucket of water swung overhead. Worked JEE examples: angle at which the string slackens for $u = \sqrt{3gR}$ ($\cos\theta = -1/3$ from the bottom); tension difference; minimum height for a loop-the-loop track ($h = 5R/2$, previewed and completed in 5.4). Misconceptions killed: "at the top of the loop the net force is zero" and "the string slackens only at the very top" |
| 4.7 | `chapter-4-mastery` | Chapter 4 Mastery | Friction's ceiling and the inward net force | Info callout "Chapter 4 in N lines". Mixed mastery (8–12): friction value below and above $\mu_s N$, least pulling force at an angle, incline at rest or sliding, block-on-block slip test, flat curve and banked curve speeds, conical pendulum, vertical-circle tension and slack angle; JEE-Advanced flavour: block on a rough rotating platform connected by a string through the centre to a hanging mass (range of $\omega$), or a block-on-block on a rough floor |

## Chapter 5: Work, Energy and Power

**Purpose:** the second great tool after Newton's laws. Define work (constant and variable forces), derive the work-energy theorem from $F = mv\frac{dv}{dx}$, separate conservative from non-conservative forces, read potential-energy curves, and finish with power.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 5.1 | `work-done-by-a-constant-force` | Work Done by a Constant Force | $W = \vec F\cdot\vec s = Fs\cos\theta$: only the part of the force along the displacement does work | Hook: carrying a heavy bag across a level floor does no work on the bag (by you, at constant velocity) yet tires you out. Interactive: `vec-canvas-2d` (mode `dot`, labels $\vec F$, $\vec s$) with the sign flipping at 90°. Work by gravity, normal force, tension, friction on an incline (each sign explained); total work = sum of works = work by the net force. Work depends on the frame. Worked JEE examples: block pulled up a rough incline by a rope (work by each force); $W = \vec F\cdot\vec s$ with $\vec F = 3\hat i + 4\hat j$, $\vec s = 2\hat i - \hat j$; work by static friction on a box in an accelerating truck (positive!). Misconceptions killed: "work by friction is always negative" and "no motion ⇒ no work done by anything" (true for that body, but check each force's displacement) |
| 5.2 | `work-done-by-a-variable-force` | Work Done by a Variable Force | $W = \int\vec F\cdot d\vec s$ = area under $F$-$x$ | Slice the path into small steps, add $F\,\Delta x$, take the limit. Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `spring`, `mu` pinned at 0, showGraph): the shaded area under the $F$-$x$ line is the work by the spring; derive $W_{\text{spring}} = \tfrac12kx_1^2 - \tfrac12kx_2^2$ from the triangle. Worked JEE examples: $F = (3x^2 + 2)$ N from $x = 1$ to 2 m (9 J); work from a piecewise $F$-$x$ graph (areas with signs); work stretching a spring from 2 cm to 4 cm vs 0 to 2 cm (three times as much); spring cut into halves has double $k$. Misconception killed: "work by a spring is $kx \cdot x$" (the force grows from zero, so it is half that) |
| 5.3 | `the-work-energy-theorem` | The Work–Energy Theorem | $W_{\text{all forces}} = \Delta K$, derived from $F = mv\frac{dv}{dx}$ | Derivation: $\int F\,dx = \int mv\,dv = \tfrac12mv^2 - \tfrac12mu^2$. Kinetic energy defined; relation $K = p^2/2m$. Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `spring`, with friction $\mu = 0.1$): energy bars show spring energy → KE + heat; $v_{\max}$ where $kx$ balances friction, not at $x = 0$; stopping point $x_0 - 2\mu mg/k$. Worked JEE examples: stopping distance of a car from $\tfrac12mv^2 = \mu mgd$ (again ∝ $v^2$, now in one line); bullet through planks (each plank removes the same KE); block sliding down a rough curved track (work by friction from $\Delta K$); chain pulled onto a table. Misconception killed: "the work-energy theorem only works for constant forces" (it is the integral of Newton's second law, valid always) |
| 5.4 | `conservative-forces-and-mechanical-energy` | Conservative Forces and Conservation of Mechanical Energy | Work by a conservative force is path-independent, so it can be stored as $U$; then $K + U$ is constant | Path-independence test (gravity: any path from A to B gives $mgh$; friction: longer path, more work). Definition $\Delta U = -W_{\text{cons}}$ and $F = -\frac{dU}{dx}$. Gravitational PE $mgh$ (near Earth), spring PE $\tfrac12kx^2$. Conservation of mechanical energy derived from the work-energy theorem; with non-conservative forces $W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta(K + U)$. Interactive: `mfe-force-lab` (mode `vertical-circle`, showGraph): KE and PE bars trade off round the loop; the Chapter 4 result $v^2 = u^2 - 2gR(1 - \cos\theta)$ now falls out of energy in one line. Worked JEE examples: loop-the-loop minimum release height $5R/2$; block compressing a spring after sliding down a smooth ramp; pendulum speed at the bottom; bead on a wire. Misconception killed: "potential energy belongs to the body alone" (it belongs to the system; only differences are physical) |
| 5.5 | `potential-energy-curves-and-equilibrium` | Potential Energy Curves and Equilibrium | $F = -\frac{dU}{dx}$: the force points downhill on the $U$ graph; minima are stable | Read a $U(x)$ graph like a roller coaster: with total energy $E$, the particle is confined to $U(x) \le E$ (turning points where $K = 0$). Equilibrium where $\frac{dU}{dx} = 0$: stable (minimum, $\frac{d^2U}{dx^2} > 0$), unstable (maximum), neutral (flat). Interactive: `graph-explorer` on $U(x) = x^4 - 4x^2$ (double well) with a draggable point, then `secant-explorer` on the same curve to see the slope (the force is minus the slope). Worked JEE examples: equilibrium points and their nature for $U = \frac{a}{x^{12}} - \frac{b}{x^6}$ (Lennard-Jones, $x_0 = (2a/b)^{1/6}$); force from $U = 3x^2 - 2x^3$; allowed region for $E = -2$ J on a given curve. Misconception killed: "at a point where $U$ is zero the force is zero" (force depends on the slope, not the value) |
| 5.6 | `power` | Power | $P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \vec F\cdot\vec v$ | Average and instantaneous power; kilowatt-hour vs kilowatt; horsepower. Derivation of $P = \vec F\cdot\vec v$ from $dW = \vec F\cdot d\vec s$. Constant-power motion: $F = P/v$ so acceleration falls as speed rises; `graph-explorer` of $F = P/v$ (expr `2000/x`, window $v$ from 1 to 40). Top speed of a car up an incline: $P = (mg\sin\theta + f)v_{\max}$. Pumps and cranes: power to lift water at a rate, efficiency. Worked JEE examples: power of a 1000 kg car at 20 m/s against 500 N of resistance (10 kW); motor raising 50 kg through 10 m in 20 s; engine pumping water out of a well; power delivered by a variable force at time $t$. Misconception killed: "more power means more force" (at high speed the same power gives less force) |
| 5.7 | `chapter-5-mastery` | Chapter 5 Mastery | Full-course diagnostic | Info callout "Chapter 5 in N lines". Mixed mastery (8–12) across the chapter and course: sign of work by each force, area under $F$-$x$, spring work between two extensions, work-energy stopping distance, loop-the-loop height, $F = -dU/dx$ and equilibrium nature, power at constant speed on an incline; JEE-Advanced flavour: block released on a spring on a rough floor (where it stops, how many passes), or a particle in a potential well with given total energy (turning points and max speed) |
