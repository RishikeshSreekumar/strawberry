# Course: Optics and Modern Physics, Rays, Waves and Quanta

**Slug:** `optics-and-modern-physics` (course 5 of the JEE physics set, after `electricity-and-magnetism`; same block schema, same seed-script pattern. File/quiz prefix `omp`, exports `ompChapter{N}Lessons`, content files `src/modules/content/omp-chapter-{0..5}-content.ts`.)

**Purpose:** Build the intuition that light is described by three models, each right in its own regime: *rays* (when everything is much bigger than the wavelength), *waves* (when slits, films and apertures are comparable to the wavelength) and *photons* (when light meets single electrons). Then carry the photon idea over to matter: electrons have wavelengths, atoms have quantised levels, nuclei store energy as missing mass, and a crystal's energy bands make the semiconductor devices every circuit relies on. Depth: CBSE Class 12 and JEE Main + Advanced.

**End state:** Given any optics or modern-physics question, the student can rebuild the answer from:

```text
rays:    reflection (i = r) + Snell (n1 sin i = n2 sin r) + sign convention
           → mirror formula, refraction at a surface, lens maker, thin lens, prism, instruments
waves:   Huygens + superposition (path difference → phase difference)
           → YDSE, thin films, single slit, resolving power, polarisation
quanta:  E = hν = 1240/λ(nm) eV,  p = h/λ,  energy conservation
           → photoelectric equation, de Broglie, Bohr levels, X-ray cut-off, E = Δm c², band gaps
```

They should never need a memorised "image position table" or a list of photoelectric graph shapes. They should be able to say *why* the lens formula has a minus sign where the mirror formula has a plus (lens: light goes through, v measured on the far side), and *why* stopping potential ignores brightness (one photon, one electron).

**Three threads run through every chapter**
1. **Intuition + visualization:** every result is first seen on a ray bench, a fringe pattern or an energy-level ladder that moves (drag the object through F, tilt the incidence past the critical angle, turn the wavelength down past threshold) before it becomes a formula.
2. **Derivation drill:** results are derived from a small core (laws of reflection and Snell's law, Huygens and superposition, E = hν and conservation of energy/momentum, Coulomb's law + quantised angular momentum), never stated cold. Each chapter's mastery lesson hides the formula sheet.
3. **JEE pay-off:** every lesson has at least one JEE-Main-style numerical with numbered steps and "*Why this step:*" notes; every mastery lesson includes JEE-Advanced-flavoured multi-step problems (combinations of optical elements, fringe shifts with slabs, photoelectric + de Broglie chains, Bohr + recoil, decay chains, diode circuits).

**Conventions used throughout:** New Cartesian sign convention (light travels left to right, distances from the pole/optical centre, along the incident light positive, heights up positive). $hc = 1240$ eV·nm, $h = 6.63\times10^{-34}$ J·s, $e = 1.6\times10^{-19}$ C, $c = 3\times10^8$ m/s, $m_e = 9.1\times10^{-31}$ kg, $1\text{ u} = 931.5$ MeV/$c^2$. Say which constants a problem uses.

## Chapter 0: Reflection and Refraction

**Slug:** `reflection-and-refraction`

**Purpose:** establish the ray model, the two laws (reflection, Snell) and the New Cartesian sign convention; derive the mirror formula and the single-surface refraction formula from geometry; master critical angle and apparent depth, the two refraction results JEE asks every year.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 0.1 | `rays-and-plane-mirrors` | Rays and Plane Mirrors | Light travels in straight lines; angle of incidence equals angle of reflection; a plane mirror's image is as far behind as the object is in front | Hook: why can you see your whole body in a mirror half your height? Ray model and when it works (objects ≫ λ). Laws of reflection. Image by extending reflected rays: virtual, erect, lateral inversion. Interactive: `omp-ray-bench` (mode `plane-mirror`, objectDistance 5–40 cm), drag the object, the reflected rays diverge from a point at $v = -u$. Derivations: rotating the mirror by $\theta$ turns the reflected ray by $2\theta$; minimum mirror height = half the person's height (similar triangles); images in two inclined mirrors, $n = 360°/\theta - 1$ (when $360/\theta$ is even). Worked examples: velocity of image when mirror/object moves (image moves at $2v$ relative to ground for a moving mirror), number of images at 60° and 72°. Misconceptions killed: "you need a mirror as tall as you are" and "the image is on the mirror's surface" |
| 0.2 | `spherical-mirrors-and-sign-convention` | Spherical Mirrors and the Sign Convention | Pole, centre, focus; $f = R/2$ for paraxial rays; one sign convention for everything | Hook: a car's side mirror says "objects are closer than they appear". Anatomy: pole P, centre of curvature C, principal axis, focus F. Derivation of $f = R/2$ from the isosceles triangle formed by a parallel ray, the normal CM and the reflected ray (small-angle approximation). New Cartesian sign convention with a picture: distances measured from P, along incident light positive, heights up positive; so $f < 0$ for concave, $f > 0$ for convex. The three principal rays (parallel → through F; through F → parallel; to the pole → symmetric). Interactive: `omp-ray-bench` (mode `concave-mirror`, all three rays), then (mode `convex-mirror`). Misconceptions killed: "$f = R/2$ holds for every ray" (only paraxial; spherical aberration) and "u is positive because distances are positive" |
| 0.3 | `mirror-formula-and-magnification` | The Mirror Formula and Magnification | $\frac1v + \frac1u = \frac1f$ and $m = -\frac vu$, derived from similar triangles | Derivation of the mirror formula from two pairs of similar triangles (object/image with P, and with F) using signed distances. Magnification $m = h'/h = -v/u$. Interactive: `omp-ray-bench` (mode `concave-mirror`, objectDistance 5–60, focalLength locked at 15 by `{min:15,max:15,...}`), drag the object from beyond C through C, F and inside F; students fill the image-nature table themselves from what they see. Misconceptions killed: "a concave mirror always forms a real image" and "magnification greater than 1 means the image is real" |
| 0.4 | `refraction-and-snells-law` | Refraction and Snell's Law | Light slows in a medium; $n = c/v$; $n_1\sin i = n_2 \sin r$ | Hook: a straw looks bent in a glass of water. Refractive index as a speed ratio, relative index $n_{21} = n_2/n_1$. Snell's law stated and motivated by the "marching band" picture (full Huygens derivation deferred to 2.1). Frequency is fixed by the source, so $\lambda$ changes: $\lambda_m = \lambda/n$. Interactive: `omp-ray-bench` (mode `refraction`, n1 locked at 1, n2 slider 1–2.5, incidence 0–89), see bending towards the normal; then swap to n1 = 1.5, n2 = 1. Lateral shift through a glass slab, $d = \frac{t\sin(i - r)}{\cos r}$, derived from the right triangle inside the slab; emergent ray parallel to incident ray. Worked JEE numericals: angle of refraction in glass; lateral shift for $t = 6$ cm, $i = 60°$, $n = \sqrt3$. Misconceptions killed: "light always bends towards the normal" and "the frequency changes in the new medium" |
| 0.5 | `apparent-depth` | Apparent Depth and Normal Shift | Objects in a denser medium look nearer the surface: $d' = d\,n_1/n_2$ near the normal | Hook: a swimming pool looks shallower than it is. Derivation of $d' = d/n$ from Snell with small angles ($\tan \approx \sin$). Normal shift $\Delta = t(1 - 1/n)$ for a slab, independent of where the object is; several slabs add their shifts. Interactive: `omp-ray-bench` (mode `apparent-depth`, n2 = 1.33, depth 5–40, viewAngle 0–70), tilt the viewing ray and see the image rise further at slant viewing. Bird–fish problems solved both ways: an object in water seen from air appears at $d/n$, an object in air seen from water appears at $n d$ (farther). Worked JEE numericals: coin under 20 cm water ($d' = 15$ cm); ink spot under a 3 cm glass slab on a 4 cm water layer; microscope refocusing distance. Misconceptions killed: "apparent depth is always $d/n$ whatever the viewing angle" and "an object in air viewed from water looks nearer" |
| 0.6 | `total-internal-reflection` | Total Internal Reflection | From denser to rarer, beyond $\theta_c = \sin^{-1}(n_2/n_1)$ no light is refracted | Hook: a diamond sparkles, and a fibre carries light round corners. Derivation: set $r = 90°$ in Snell. Interactive: `omp-ray-bench` (mode `refraction`, n1 slider 1.3–2.5, n2 locked at 1, incidence 0–89), push $i$ past $\theta_c$ and watch the refracted ray vanish and the reflected ray brighten. Applications: optical fibres (acceptance angle $\sin\alpha_{\max} = \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2}$ derived), totally reflecting prisms, mirage, sparkle of diamond ($\theta_c \approx 24.4°$). Circle of light: a point source at depth $h$ lights a disc of radius $R = h\tan\theta_c = h/\sqrt{n^2 - 1}$ on the surface. Worked JEE numericals: critical angle for glass–water; radius of the lit circle for a bulb 4 m under water ($n = 4/3$, $R \approx 4.54$ m). Misconceptions killed: "TIR can happen going from air into glass" and "at the critical angle all light is already reflected" |
| 0.7 | `refraction-at-spherical-surfaces` | Refraction at a Spherical Surface | $\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}$ | Derivation from Snell at a point on the surface with small angles (exterior-angle theorem on the triangles through O, C, I), signed $u, v, R$. Magnification $m = \frac{n_1 v}{n_2 u}$. Special cases: $R \to \infty$ gives apparent depth back (consistency check); the formula reduces to the mirror formula when $n_2 = -n_1$ (trick callout, optional). Glass sphere and fish-bowl problems: fish at the centre of a bowl appears at the centre (magnified by $n$); a mark on a glass sphere seen through it. Worked JEE numericals: object 20 cm in front of a convex glass surface $R = 10$ cm ($n = 1.5$); fish 10 cm from the wall of a bowl of radius 15 cm. Misconceptions killed: "the sign of R is decided by whether the surface looks convex" (it is decided by where C lies relative to the incident light) and "the formula needs $n_1 < n_2$" |
| 0.8 | `chapter-0-mastery` | Chapter 0 Mastery | Can the student trace, sign and solve any mirror or interface problem? | "Chapter in N lines" info callout. Mixed mastery quizzes: images in inclined mirrors, rotation of reflected ray, image nature at a given $u$ without a table, mirror formula with correct signs, velocity of image, Snell and lateral shift, apparent depth through two layers, critical angle and lit circle, single spherical surface, JEE-Advanced style: a concave mirror at the bottom of a water-filled vessel (refraction + reflection + refraction) |

## Chapter 1: Lenses, Prisms and Optical Instruments

**Slug:** `lenses-prisms-and-instruments`

**Purpose:** build a thin lens from two refracting surfaces, derive the lens maker and thin-lens formulas, combine lenses, trace rays through a prism to minimum deviation and dispersion, and use it all to design the eye's corrections, the microscope and the telescope.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 1.1 | `lens-makers-formula` | Two Surfaces Make a Lens | Apply the single-surface formula twice: $\frac1f = (n-1)\left(\frac1{R_1} - \frac1{R_2}\right)$ | Hook: why does a magnifying glass burn paper at one particular distance? Thin-lens idealisation. Derivation: image by the first surface becomes the object for the second, thickness neglected, add the two equations. Relative index $n = n_{\text{lens}}/n_{\text{medium}}$: a glass lens in water has a longer focal length; an air bubble in water acts as a diverging lens. Sign practice for biconvex, plano-convex, concavo-convex (meniscus) lenses. | 1.2 | `thin-lens-formula` | The Thin Lens Formula and Images | $\frac1v - \frac1u = \frac1f$, $m = \frac vu$ | Derivation from the lens maker (object at $u$, image at $v$ via both surfaces), and separately from similar triangles with the ray through O and the ray through F. The three principal rays through a lens. | 1.6 | `the-eye-and-simple-microscope` | The Eye and the Simple Microscope | The eye is an adjustable lens; magnifiers increase the angle an object subtends | Eye as a converging lens of variable focal length, near point $D = 25$ cm, far point at infinity; accommodation. | 1.7 | `microscopes-and-telescopes` | Compound Microscope and Telescopes | Two lenses: an objective makes an image, the eyepiece magnifies it | Compound microscope: $M = m_o M_e = \frac{v_o}{u_o}\left(1 + \frac{D}{f_e}\right)$ (near point) and $\approx \frac{L}{f_o}\frac{D}{f_e}$ (at infinity), tube length. ## Chapter 2: Wave Optics

**Slug:** `wave-optics`

**Purpose:** justify the ray laws from Huygens' wavefronts, then show what rays cannot explain: interference (YDSE and thin films), diffraction (single slit and resolving power) and polarisation (Malus and Brewster).

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 2.1 | `wavefronts-and-huygens-principle` | Wavefronts and Huygens' Principle | Every point on a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets; the envelope is the new wavefront | Hook: rays are perpendicular to wavefronts, so why did we need rays at all? Wavefronts: spherical, cylindrical, plane; rays as normals. Huygens construction. Derivations: law of reflection (congruent triangles) and Snell's law ($\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}$) from the wavefront crossing the boundary. Frequency unchanged, $\lambda$ and $v$ change. Wavefront through a prism, lens, concave mirror (qualitative pictures). Interactive: `omp-ray-bench` (mode `refraction`) revisited, with a text prompt linking $\sin i/\sin r$ to the speed ratio. Worked example: speed and wavelength of 600 nm light in glass of $n = 1.5$ (400 nm, $2\times10^8$ m/s). Misconception killed: "wavelength stays the same in glass because the colour does" |
| 2.2 | `superposition-and-coherence` | Superposition and Coherent Sources | Amplitudes add; intensities add only for incoherent sources | Two waves of amplitude $a_1, a_2$ and phase difference $\phi$: $A^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + 2a_1a_2\cos\phi$ (phasor/law of cosines), so $I = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\phi$. Phase difference $= \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\times$ path difference. Constructive/destructive conditions; $\frac{I_{\max}}{I_{\min}} = \left(\frac{\sqrt{I_1} + \sqrt{I_2}}{\sqrt{I_1} - \sqrt{I_2}}\right)^2$. Coherence: why two bulbs give no fringes (phase relation changes every $10^{-8}$ s, $\cos\phi$ averages to 0). Interactive: `transform-playground` of $I(\phi)$ with $I_1$, $I_2$ sliders (config below); `sinusoid-playground` to show two waves in and out of phase. Worked JEE numericals: amplitude ratio 2:1 gives $I_{\max}/I_{\min} = 9$; intensity at a point where path difference is $\lambda/3$. Misconceptions killed: "two torches can make an interference pattern" and "at a dark fringe energy is destroyed" (it is redistributed to the bright fringes) |
| 2.3 | `youngs-double-slit-experiment` | Young's Double-Slit Experiment | Path difference $\frac{yd}{D}$ → fringes of width $\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$ | Hook: Young's 1801 experiment settled the wave nature of light. Derivation of path difference $S_2P - S_1P \approx d\sin\theta \approx yd/D$ (with the $D \gg d$ approximation stated). Bright at $y_n = n\lambda D/d$, dark at $(n + \tfrac12)\lambda D/d$, fringe width $\beta$, angular fringe width $\lambda/d$. Intensity $I = 4I_0\cos^2\frac{\pi y}{\beta}$. Interactive: `transform-playground` of the intensity on the screen with sliders for $\beta$ and $I_0$ (config below). Worked JEE numericals: $\lambda = 600$ nm, $d = 0.5$ mm, $D = 1$ m ($\beta = 1.2$ mm); position of the 3rd dark fringe; which change doubles $\beta$. Misconceptions killed: "moving the slits apart makes the fringes wider" and "fringes get wider far from the centre" (equally spaced in the small-angle regime) |
| 2.4 | `fringe-shifts-and-thin-films` | Fringe Shifts, Two Wavelengths and Thin Films | Anything that adds optical path shifts the pattern; films interfere with themselves | YDSE variations: whole apparatus in a liquid ($\beta' = \beta/n$), thin slab of thickness $t$ over one slit (extra path $(\mu - 1)t$, shift $= (\mu - 1)tD/d$, towards the covered slit; fringe width unchanged), source shifted sideways, white-light fringes (central white, coloured edges), coincidence of two wavelengths ($n_1\lambda_1 = n_2\lambda_2$). Thin films (intro): reflection from a denser medium adds $\pi$; reflected-light conditions $2\mu t\cos r = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda$ bright, $m\lambda$ dark; soap bubbles, oil films, anti-reflection coating ($\mu t = \lambda/4$). Interactive: `transform-playground` with a shift parameter $s$ in $4\cos^2\frac{\pi(y - s)}{\beta}$. Worked JEE numericals: mica sheet shifting the central fringe by 5 fringes gives $t$; smallest overlap of 500 nm and 600 nm bright fringes; minimum coating thickness for 550 nm on glass with $\mu = 1.38$. Misconceptions killed: "a slab over one slit changes the fringe width" and "the thinnest part of a soap film looks bright" (it looks dark because of the $\pi$ shift) |
| 2.5 | `single-slit-diffraction` | Single-Slit Diffraction | Pair up the slit's halves: minima at $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$ | Hook: sound bends round doors, light hardly does. Derivation of the first minimum by pairing points $a/2$ apart; general minima $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$; secondary maxima near $(m + \tfrac12)\lambda$ with falling intensity. Central maximum angular width $2\lambda/a$, linear width $2\lambda D/a$. Fresnel distance $z_F = a^2/\lambda$ (when ray optics is valid). Interference vs diffraction comparison table (equal fringes vs a wide central band). Interactive: `transform-playground` of the $\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2$ pattern with a slit-width slider (config below). Worked JEE numericals: slit 0.2 mm, 600 nm, screen 1 m (central width 6 mm); slit width for which the first minimum is at 30°; YDSE fringes inside a single-slit envelope (how many bright fringes in the central maximum, $2d/a - 1$). Misconceptions killed: "the central maximum is the same width as the others" (twice) and "a narrower slit gives a narrower pattern" |
| 2.6 | `resolving-power` | Diffraction Limits: Resolving Power | Two points are just resolved when one's central maximum sits on the other's first minimum | Rayleigh criterion. Angular limit of a circular aperture $\theta \approx 1.22\lambda/D$; resolving power of a telescope $\propto D/\lambda$ and of a microscope $\frac{2n\sin\beta}{1.22\lambda}$ (NCERT form). Why bigger telescope mirrors and why electron microscopes (link forward to de Broglie in 3.5). Interactive: `transform-playground` sketch of two overlapping diffraction peaks with a separation slider (config below). Worked JEE numericals: minimum angular separation for a 1 m telescope at 500 nm; distance at which a car's headlights (1.2 m apart) are just resolved by the eye (pupil 2 mm). Misconception killed: "more magnification always shows more detail" (resolution is set by aperture and wavelength) |
| 2.7 | `polarisation` | Polarisation | Light is a transverse wave; a polaroid passes only one direction of $\vec E$ | Hook: why polaroid sunglasses cut glare from water. Transverse waves and planes of vibration; unpolarised light through one polaroid gives $I_0/2$. Malus' law $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$ derived from the component of $\vec E$. Three-polaroid problem (crossed polaroids with one at 45° between: $I_0/8$). Brewster's law $\tan\theta_B = n$ derived from reflected ⊥ refracted ($\theta_B + r = 90°$), polarisation by scattering (sky at 90° to the sun). Interactive: `graph-explorer` of $\cos^2\theta$ (θ in degrees scaled, config below) with a draggable analyser angle.
