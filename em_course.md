# Course: Electricity and Magnetism, Fields That Push, Store and Induce

**Slug:** `electricity-and-magnetism` (course 4 of the JEE physics set, alongside `motion-forces-energy`, `momentum-rotation-gravitation`, `oscillations-waves-thermal` and `optics-and-modern-physics`; same block schema, same seed-script pattern as the maths courses.)

**Purpose:** Build the intuition that a charge changes the space around it (a field), that the field can be read either as arrows (force per unit charge) or as heights (potential), and that moving charges make a second field which, when it changes, makes the first one again. Then build the JEE Main + Advanced fluency on top of that picture: Coulomb and superposition, Gauss's law, potential and energy, capacitor networks, DC circuits and instruments, magnetic forces and Biot–Savart/Ampère, induction, transients and AC. Depth: CBSE Class 12 plus JEE Advanced.

**End state:** Given any E&M question, the student can reconstruct the answer from:

```text
charge → field E (force per coulomb) → potential V (energy per coulomb)
   Gauss:     flux out = q_enclosed / ε₀        (symmetry → E in one line)
   circuits:  KCL (charge conserved), KVL (V is a height: loops return to start)
moving charge → field B → force q v × B,  I l × B
   Faraday:   emf = −dΦ/dt  (Lenz = energy conservation)
   AC:        rotating phasors; Z = √(R² + (X_L − X_C)²)
```

They should never need a memorised list of "field of a ring", "capacitor with a slab" or "LCR phase" results. They should be able to say *why* the field of a sheet does not fall off with distance (Gauss, flat symmetry), *why* half the battery's energy is lost when a capacitor charges (whatever R is), and *why* the current in an inductor cannot jump (it would need infinite emf).

**Three threads run through every chapter**
1. **Intuition + visualization:** every field, potential, circuit and phasor appears first as a picture that moves (drag a charge and watch the field lines rearrange, slide R and watch τ stretch, spin ωt and watch phasors rotate) before it appears as a formula.
2. **Derivation drill:** results are derived from a small core (Coulomb + superposition, Gauss's law, V as work per unit charge, charge and energy conservation in circuits, the Lorentz force, Biot–Savart/Ampère, Faraday–Lenz), never stated cold. Each chapter's mastery lesson hides the formula sheet.
3. **JEE pay-off:** every lesson carries at least one JEE-Main-style numerical with numbered steps and *Why this step:* notes; every mastery lesson has JEE-Advanced-flavoured multi-step problems (networks, conductors, rods on rails, LCR phasors). SI units throughout, $k = 9\times10^9$ N m² C⁻², $e = 1.6\times10^{-19}$ C, $g = 10$ m/s² unless stated.

Content files: `src/modules/content/em-chapter-{0..5}-content.ts`, exports `emChapter{N}Lessons`. Quiz ids: `em{chapter}-{lesson}-q{n}` (e.g. `em2-4-q3`). No video blocks yet.

## Chapter 0: Electric Charge and Field

**Purpose:** establish charge as a conserved, quantised property; master Coulomb's law as a vector law and superposition; introduce the field as force per unit charge, read it through field lines, apply it to the dipole and to continuous charge.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 0.1 | `electric-charge` | Electric Charge | Charge is quantised ($q = ne$), conserved and additive; objects charge by friction, conduction and induction | Hook: a rubbed balloon sticks to a *neutral* wall. Why? (induced polarisation, the near side is attracted more than the far side is repelled). Two kinds of charge, conductors vs insulators, charging by induction step by step (bring rod near, earth, remove earth, remove rod). Worked examples: electrons in 1 μC ($6.25\times10^{12}$), net charge after rubbing (mass transferred is negligible), two identical conducting spheres touched and separated (charges average). Misconceptions killed: "a neutral body contains no charge" and "a charge of $2.4\times10^{-19}$ C is possible" |
| 0.2 | `coulombs-law` | Coulomb's Law | $F = \dfrac{k\lvert q_1 q_2\rvert}{r^2}$ along the line joining them; $k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}$ | Vector form $\vec F_{12} = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}\hat r_{12}$ and Newton's third law. Electric vs gravitational force between electron and proton (ratio about $10^{39}$). Force in a medium $F/K$. Interactive: `em-field-canvas` (mode `force`, two charges, `chargeSlider` −5…5 μC): drag them apart and watch the force fall as $1/r^2$; flip a sign and watch repulsion become attraction. Worked examples: halving $r$, touch-and-separate then new force, two suspended pith balls (tan θ = F/mg). Misconception killed: "the bigger charge pushes harder on the smaller one" (equal and opposite) |
| 0.3 | `superposition-of-forces` | Superposition of Forces | Each pair acts as if the others were absent; the net force is the vector sum | Components method, symmetry shortcuts (square, equilateral triangle, centre of a regular polygon: net zero). Interactive: `em-field-canvas` (mode `force`, three charges, `forceOn: 0`), dashed forces from each charge and their vector sum drawn to scale. Worked examples: net force on a corner charge of a square; where to place $q_3$ so it is in equilibrium between $+4q$ and $+q$ separated by 3 m (1 m from $+q$). Misconception killed: "net force = sum of magnitudes" |
| 0.4 | `electric-field` | The Electric Field | $\vec E = \vec F/q_0$ (test-charge limit); point charge $E = kq/r^2$; fields superpose | Field as a property of the space, independent of the test charge. | 0.5 | `field-lines` | Field Lines | Lines start on +, end on −, never cross; density shows strength; number of lines ∝ charge | Rules derived from what E is (single-valued, so no crossing). Concept quizzes: read relative charge magnitudes and signs from a line picture; where is the field strongest. Misconceptions killed: "field lines are the paths charged particles follow" and "a region with no lines drawn has no field" |
| 0.6 | `electric-dipole` | The Electric Dipole | $p = q(2a)$ from − to +; axial $E = \dfrac{2kp}{r^3}$, equatorial $E = \dfrac{kp}{r^3}$ opposite to $\vec p$ | Derive both fields from superposition and the far-field limit $r \gg a$. Dipole in a uniform field: net force zero, torque $\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E$, energy $U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$, stable (θ = 0) and unstable (θ = 180°) equilibrium, work to rotate $W = pE(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2)$, small oscillations preview. Interactives: `em-field-canvas` (mode `field-vectors`, readouts include `dipole`); `transform-playground` exact vs point-dipole axial field (slider shrinks $a$ at fixed $p$; the curves merge). Misconception killed: "a dipole in a uniform field accelerates" (it only turns) |
| 0.7 | `continuous-charge-distributions` | Continuous Charge Distributions | Chop into $dq = \lambda\,dl$ (or $\sigma\,dA$, $\rho\,dV$), add the fields with symmetry | Ring on its axis: $E = \dfrac{kQx}{(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}$, zero at the centre, maximum at $x = R/\sqrt2$; semicircular arc at its centre $\dfrac{2k\lambda}{R}$; finite line (perpendicular point) $\dfrac{k\lambda}{d}(\sin\alpha + \sin\beta)$, infinite line $\dfrac{2k\lambda}{d}$; disc on axis stated as a ring sum. Interactive: `transform-playground` ring-axis field with $Q$ and $R$ sliders; the peak slides to $R/\sqrt2$. Worked examples: field at the centre of a semicircular ring; field on the axis of a ring at $x = R$; symmetry kills perpendicular components. Misconception killed: "the field is greatest at the centre of a charged ring" (it is zero there) |
| 0.8 | `chapter-0-mastery` | Chapter 0 Mastery | Can the student go from charges to forces to fields without a formula sheet? | "Chapter in 8 lines" callout. 8–12 mastery quizzes: quantisation, touch-and-share, three-charge equilibrium, net force on a square corner, neutral point, field-line reading, dipole torque and work, ring-axis maximum; one JEE-Advanced style: pendulum bob with charge in a uniform horizontal field (new equilibrium angle and period) |

## Chapter 1: Gauss's Law and Electric Potential

**Purpose:** two new ways of reading the field. Flux and Gauss's law turn symmetric problems into one line; potential turns vectors into scalars and forces into energy. Finish with conductors, where both ideas meet.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 1.1 | `electric-flux` | Electric Flux | $\Phi = \vec E\cdot\vec A = EA\cos\theta$: how much field pierces a surface | Hook: rain through a tilted window. Area vector, outward normal on closed surfaces, flux through a surface in a uniform field (in = out, net zero). Interactive: `vec-canvas-2d` (mode `dot`, `a` = $\vec E$, `b` = $\vec A$): tilt the area and watch the flux change sign at 90°. Worked examples: flux through a tilted square, through a hemisphere in a uniform field ($\pi R^2 E$ through the flat face). Misconception killed: "zero net flux means zero field on the surface" |
| 1.2 | `gauss-law` | Gauss's Law | $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$ | Derivation for a point charge and a sphere, then any closed surface (flux counts lines; lines from outside charges enter and leave). Charge at the centre of a cube (each face $q/6\varepsilon_0$), at a corner ($q/8\varepsilon_0$ through the cube: $q/24\varepsilon_0$ through each of the three far faces, zero through the three faces that touch the corner), at the centre of a face ($q/2\varepsilon_0$). Worked examples as above. Misconception killed: "the $\vec E$ in Gauss's law is produced by the enclosed charge only" (it is the total field; only the *net flux* ignores outside charges) |
| 1.3 | `applications-of-gauss-law` | Applying Gauss's Law | Symmetry + a clever surface gives E in one line | Infinite line $E = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ (cylinder), infinite sheet $\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$ (pillbox), two parallel sheets (field only between for $\pm\sigma$), spherical shell (0 inside, $kQ/r^2$ outside), uniformly charged solid sphere ($kQr/R^3$ inside). Interactive: `family-gallery` comparing $1/r^2$ (point/sphere), $1/r$ (line) and constant (sheet). Worked examples: field between and outside two sheets with $+\sigma$ and $-\sigma$ and with $+\sigma$, $+\sigma$; ratio of fields at $R/2$ and $2R$ from a solid sphere (2:1). Misconception killed: "the field of a big charged sheet weakens as you move away" |
| 1.4 | `electric-potential` | Electric Potential | $V = W_{\infty\to P}/q_0$; point charge $V = kq/r$; potentials add as scalars | Derive $V = kq/r$ by integrating $E$ from infinity; potential difference $V_A - V_B = \int_A^B \vec E\cdot d\vec l$; $E = -dV/dr$. Potential of a dipole ($kp\cos\theta/r^2$), ring axis, shell (constant inside). Interactives: `em-field-canvas` (mode `equipotentials`, readouts `potential`, `field`); `transform-playground` $V \propto q/r$ vs $E \propto q/r^2$. Worked examples: V at the centre of a square of charges, point on the axis of a ring, where on the line of $+q$ and $-2q$ is $V = 0$ (two points). Misconceptions killed: "where E = 0, V must be 0" (midpoint of two like charges) and its converse (midpoint of a dipole) |
| 1.5 | `equipotential-surfaces` | Equipotential Surfaces | Surfaces of constant V; always perpendicular to field lines; crowded where E is strong | No work along an equipotential. Uniform field: $V = -Ed$, only the displacement along $\vec E$ counts. $E_x = -\partial V/\partial x$ from a given $V(x, y)$. Interactive: `em-field-canvas` (mode `equipotentials`, `showLines: true`) with a single charge then a dipole: lines cut every contour at 90°. Worked examples: $V = 3x^2 + 2y$ → $\vec E = -(6x\hat i + 2\hat j)$; potential difference along a slanted path in a uniform field. Misconception killed: "equipotentials of a point charge are equally spaced for equal steps of V" |
| 1.6 | `potential-energy-of-systems` | Potential Energy and Work | $U = kq_1q_2/r$ for each pair; $W_{\text{field}} = q(V_A - V_B)$, path-independent | Work to assemble 3 or 4 charges (sum over pairs), energy conservation for a charge accelerated through V ($\tfrac12 mv^2 = qV$, electron-volt), closest approach of an α-particle to a nucleus, dipole energy recap. Interactive: `em-field-canvas` (mode `work`, `testCharge: -2`): drag A and B, both dashed paths give the same work. Worked examples: work to bring $q$ to the corner of a square that already has three charges; speed of an electron after 100 V ($5.9\times10^6$ m/s); distance of closest approach. Misconception killed: "moving along a longer path takes more work in an electric field" |
| 1.7 | `conductors-in-electrostatics` | Conductors | Inside a conductor E = 0, charge sits on the surface, the whole body is one equipotential | Consequences: surface field $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ perpendicular to the surface, cavities shield, charge crowds at sharp points (lightning rods, corona), electrostatic pressure $\sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0$. Two spheres joined by a wire: equal V, $\sigma \propto 1/R$. Concentric shells: potentials by superposition; earthing an inner shell. Table: charge inside cavity / on outer surface / field outside. Worked examples: charges on two joined spheres of radii 1 cm and 3 cm; potential of inner shell after earthing. Misconception killed: "a charge placed in a cavity is hidden from outside" (the outer surface still carries $+q$; only the reverse shielding holds) |
| 1.8 | `chapter-1-mastery` | Chapter 1 Mastery | Flux, Gauss, V, U and conductors from scratch | "Chapter in 8 lines" callout. 8–12 mastery quizzes: cube-corner flux, sheets, solid-sphere ratio, V = 0 points, E from V(x, y), work to assemble, accelerated electron, joined spheres; JEE-Advanced style: concentric shells with one earthed, find charge redistribution and potentials |

## Chapter 2: Capacitors

**Purpose:** a capacitor as a device that stores charge (and energy) at a potential difference. Derive C for standard geometries, combine them, fill them with dielectrics, count the energy, share charge, and watch them charge through a resistor.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 2.1 | `capacitance` | Capacitance | $C = Q/V$ depends only on geometry and medium | Isolated sphere $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R$ (Earth ≈ 711 μF: a farad is huge). Parallel plates $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ derived from the sheet field; spherical and cylindrical capacitors stated with derivation sketch. Interactive: `function-machine` plate separation → capacitance ($A = 100$ cm², $C = 88.5/d$ pF with $d$ in mm). Worked examples: plate area needed for 1 F at 1 mm (about $1.1\times10^8$ m²); doubling $d$ and doubling $A$. Misconception killed: "a capacitor's capacitance grows if you put more charge on it" |
| 2.2 | `combinations-of-capacitors` | Series and Parallel | Series: same Q, $1/C = \sum 1/C_i$; parallel: same V, $C = \sum C_i$ | Both derived from charge conservation and KVL. Voltage division in series ($V_i \propto 1/C_i$). Bridge networks by symmetry (balanced Wheatstone of capacitors), networks between two points by redrawing. Worked examples: 2, 3, 6 μF in series (1 μF) and parallel (11 μF); charges and voltages on each in a mixed network across 12 V; equivalent C of a balanced bridge. Misconception killed: "capacitors in series share the voltage equally" |
| 2.3 | `dielectrics` | Dielectrics | A dielectric polarises, weakens the field by $K$, and raises $C$ to $K\varepsilon_0 A/d$ | Bound charges, $E = E_0/K$. Partial slab $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t + t/K}$ (conducting slab: $K\to\infty$). Slabs side by side (parallel) vs stacked (series). Table: inserting a dielectric with battery connected vs disconnected (Q, V, E, C, U). Interactive: `transform-playground` $C/C_0$ vs slab thickness fraction with a $K$ slider. Worked examples: slab of thickness $d/2$, $K = 4$ → $C = 1.6C_0$; two dielectrics filling halves side by side and stacked. Misconception killed: "inserting a dielectric always increases the stored energy" (falls if the battery is disconnected) |
| 2.4 | `energy-stored-in-capacitors` | Energy Stored | $U = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \dfrac{Q^2}{2C} = \tfrac12 QV$, derived by integrating $V\,dq$ | Energy density $u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$; force between plates $\dfrac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 A}$ (not $QE$: each plate feels the other's field only). Battery does $QV$; half is stored, half heats the wires whatever R is. Worked examples: energy change on inserting a slab in each case; work to pull plates apart; energy of a 10 μF capacitor at 100 V (0.05 J). Misconception killed: "all the battery's work ends up stored in the capacitor" |
| 2.5 | `charge-sharing` | Charge Sharing | Joining charged capacitors: charge conserved, common potential, energy lost | $V = \dfrac{C_1V_1 + C_2V_2}{C_1 + C_2}$, loss $\Delta U = \dfrac{C_1C_2(V_1 - V_2)^2}{2(C_1 + C_2)}$; reversed-polarity joining ($V_1 + V_2$ in the loss). Where the energy goes (heat, radiation). Interactive: `function-machine` energy lost when a 2 μF capacitor at 100 V is joined to an uncharged $C_2$ ($\Delta U = \dfrac{10C_2}{2 + C_2}$ mJ). Worked examples: common potential and loss; plates connected + to −. Misconception killed: "charge sharing conserves energy because no battery is involved" |
| 2.6 | `rc-circuits` | Charging and Discharging: RC Circuits | $q = CE(1 - e^{-t/RC})$, $i = \dfrac{E}{R}e^{-t/RC}$; time constant $\tau = RC$ | Derive from KVL: $E - iR - q/C = 0$, separate variables. At $t = 0$ an uncharged capacitor acts like a wire; at steady state like a break. 63% at $\tau$, half-charge at $\tau\ln2$. Interactive: `em-circuit-lab` (mode `rc`, `process: "charge"`, then `"discharge"`): slide R and C, the dashed ghost keeps the original τ. Worked examples: τ for 100 Ω and 10 μF; time to reach 90% of full charge ($\tau\ln10$); steady-state charge on a capacitor in a resistor network (JEE classic). Misconception killed: "a capacitor is fully charged after one time constant" |
| 2.7 | `chapter-2-mastery` | Chapter 2 Mastery | Store, combine, fill, share, charge | "Chapter in 7 lines" callout. 8–12 mastery quizzes: C of plates, series/parallel network, slab formula, battery connected vs disconnected, energy density, charge-sharing loss, RC time to a fraction; JEE-Advanced style: network with two capacitors and a switch, find heat generated after closing it |

## Chapter 4: Magnetic Effects of Current

**Purpose:** moving charges feel and make a second field. Start with the Lorentz force and its motion (circles, helices, crossed fields), build B from currents (Biot–Savart, then Ampère for symmetry), then forces and torques on current loops, meters, and a short tour of magnetism in matter.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 4.1 | `magnetic-force-on-a-charge` | The Magnetic Force on a Charge | $\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B$: perpendicular to both, so it never does work | Definition of the tesla from the force. Right-hand rule and the sign flip for negative charges. Full Lorentz force $q(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)$. Interactive: `vec-space-3d` (mode `cross`, caption maps $\vec a\to\vec v$, $\vec b\to\vec B$) with an angle slider: the force is largest at 90°, zero along $\vec B$. Worked examples: force on a proton at $2\times10^6$ m/s in 0.5 T at 30°; direction of force on an electron (component vectors, $\hat i\times\hat j$ table). Misconception killed: "a magnetic field can speed up a charged particle" |
| 4.2 | `motion-in-a-magnetic-field` | Circles, Helices and Crossed Fields | $r = \dfrac{mv}{qB}$, $T = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB}$ independent of speed | Derive from $qvB = mv^2/r$. Helix: pitch $\dfrac{2\pi m v\cos\theta}{qB}$. Velocity selector $v = E/B$; mass spectrometer; cyclotron (frequency, maximum KE $\dfrac{q^2B^2R^2}{2m}$). Particle entering a field region of width $d$: deviation angle. Interactive: `function-machine` proton speed ($10^6$ m/s) → radius in 0.1 T ($r = 10.4v$ cm). Worked examples: radius ratio of a proton and an α-particle with equal KE (1:1) and equal momentum (2:1); pitch of a helix. Misconception killed: "faster particles take longer to go round" |

## Chapter 5: Electromagnetic Induction and AC

**Purpose:** changing magnetic flux makes an emf. Faraday and Lenz, motional emf, inductance and energy, LR transients, then alternating current with phasors, the series LCR circuit, resonance, power and transformers, and a short close on electromagnetic waves.

| Lesson | Slug | Topic | Core idea | Planned content |
| ------ | ---- | ----- | --------- | --------------- |
| 5.1 | `faraday-and-lenz` | Faraday's and Lenz's Laws | $\varepsilon = -\dfrac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$; the induced current opposes the change that caused it | Hook: a magnet dropped through a copper tube falls slowly. Magnetic flux, ways to change it ($B$, $A$, angle). Lenz's law as energy conservation (the opposite sign would give free energy). Charge through a coil $\Delta q = \Delta\Phi/R$, independent of how fast. Interactive: `secant-explorer` on $\Phi(t) = t^2 - 4t$: the secant slope is the average emf, the limit is the instantaneous emf. Worked examples: emf in a coil of 100 turns as B falls from 0.5 T to 0 in 0.1 s; charge through a coil flipped over. Misconception killed: "a large flux means a large emf" (only a changing flux matters) |
| 5.2 | `motional-emf` | Motional EMF | A rod moving through B has $\varepsilon = Bvl$, from $q\vec v\times\vec B$ on its charges | Derivation from the Lorentz force and from flux. Rod on rails with resistor: current, retarding force $B^2l^2v/R$, terminal velocity of a falling rod $\dfrac{mgR}{B^2l^2}$, energy balance (mechanical power = $I^2R$). Rotating rod $\tfrac12 B\omega l^2$, rotating disc. Eddy currents and damping. Worked examples: terminal speed of a falling rod; emf across a rotating rod; rod given initial speed $v_0$ on rails, distance travelled before stopping $\dfrac{mv_0R}{B^2l^2}$. Misconception killed: "no closed circuit means no emf" (the emf is there; only the current needs a circuit) |
| 5.3 | `self-and-mutual-inductance` | Self and Mutual Inductance | $\Phi = LI$, $\varepsilon = -L\dfrac{dI}{dt}$; $\Phi_2 = MI_1$ | Solenoid $L = \mu_0 n^2 Al$; coaxial solenoids $M = \mu_0 n_1 n_2 A l$; $M \le \sqrt{L_1L_2}$. Energy $\tfrac12 LI^2$ derived by integrating $LI\,dI$; energy density $B^2/2\mu_0$. Inductors in series and parallel (no coupling). Worked examples: L of a 500-turn solenoid; emf when current changes 2 A in 1 ms through 50 mH (100 V); energy stored. Misconception killed: "an inductor opposes current" (it opposes a *change* in current) |
| 5.4 | `lr-circuits-and-lc-oscillations` | LR Circuits and LC Oscillations | $i = \dfrac{E}{R}(1 - e^{-Rt/L})$; $\tau = L/R$; LC circuits swap energy at $\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ | Derivation from KVL. At switch-on an inductor acts like a break, at steady state like a wire; why opening a switch sparks. LC oscillations as the electrical spring–mass: $q = Q_0\cos\omega t$, energy sloshing between $\tfrac{q^2}{2C}$ and $\tfrac12 Li^2$. Interactive: `em-circuit-lab` (mode `lr`, `process: "charge"`, then `"discharge"`). Worked examples: τ and current after 2τ; current just after and long after closing a switch in an LR network; LC maximum current from $Q_0$. Misconception killed: "the current through an inductor can change instantly" |
| 5.5 | `alternating-current-and-phasors` | Alternating Current and Phasors | $v = V_0\sin\omega t$; rms $= V_0/\sqrt2$; R, L, C alone as rotating phasors | Mean over a half cycle $2V_0/\pi$, over a full cycle 0; rms derived from the mean of $\sin^2$. Phasors: each sinusoid is the projection of a rotating arrow. Resistor in phase; inductor $X_L = \omega L$, current lags 90°; capacitor $X_C = 1/\omega C$, current leads 90° (ELI the ICE man). Interactives: `circle-to-wave` (a rotating phasor unwraps into a sine); `em-circuit-lab` (mode `ac-element`, `element` R, then L, then C): change f and watch $I_0$ change for L and C only. Worked examples: rms of 220 V mains is the rms, so peak ≈ 311 V; $X_L$ and $X_C$ at 50 Hz for 0.1 H and 10 μF (31.4 Ω, 318 Ω). Misconception killed: "the average AC voltage over a cycle is $V_0/\sqrt2$" (it is zero; rms is $V_0/\sqrt2$) |
| 5.6 | `series-lcr-and-resonance` | Series LCR and Resonance | Phasor sum gives $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$, $\tan\phi = \dfrac{X_L - X_C}{R}$; resonance at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ | Phasor diagram built step by step (current as reference). Inductive vs capacitive circuits. Resonance: $Z = R$, current maximum, $V_L = V_C$ can each exceed the source. Quality factor $Q = \dfrac{\omega_0 L}{R}$, bandwidth $\Delta\omega = R/L$, half-power points. Interactives: `em-circuit-lab` (mode `lcr`) then (mode `resonance`, R from 10 to 200 Ω). Worked examples: R = 100 Ω, L = 0.1 H, C = 10 μF on 10 V peak 50 Hz (Z ≈ 304 Ω, φ ≈ −70.8°, capacitive); resonant frequency 159 Hz; voltages across each element at resonance. Misconception killed: "$V_R + V_L + V_C$ equals the source voltage" (they add as phasors) |
| 5.7 | `ac-power-transformers-and-em-waves` | AC Power, Transformers and EM Waves | $P = V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi$; ideal transformer $V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p = I_p/I_s$ | Power factor, wattless current (pure L or C), choke coil vs resistor for dimming. Transformer principle (mutual induction), efficiency and losses (copper, eddy, hysteresis, flux leakage), why transmission uses high voltage ($I^2R$ loss). EM waves brief: displacement current $\varepsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt$, $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$, $E_0 = cB_0$, transverse, spectrum in order with uses. Interactive: `em-circuit-lab` (mode `lcr`, readouts `power`, `phase`) to see $\cos\phi$ rise to 1 at resonance. Worked examples: power drawn by an LCR circuit; transformer current ratio; line loss at 11 kV vs 220 V. Misconception killed: "a step-up transformer increases power" |
| 5.8 | `chapter-5-mastery` | Chapter 5 Mastery | Flux changes, transients, phasors and power | "Chapter in 8 lines" callout. 8–12 mastery quizzes: Lenz direction, $\Delta q = \Delta\Phi/R$, rod on rails, self-inductance energy, LR switch-on, rms vs mean, LCR impedance and phase, resonance and Q, transformer; JEE-Advanced style: falling rod reaching terminal velocity with energy bookkeeping, and an LCR problem where $V_L$ exceeds the source at resonance |
